In April, a small lab audited LoCoMo, one of the most cited benchmarks for testing whether an AI system remembers things across long conversations. A benchmark is a fixed set of questions plus an answer key, and it is how the field decides whose system is better. The auditors found 99 of the 1,540 answers in the key were wrong. The key invents facts the conversation never contained, resolves "last Saturday" to a Sunday, and puts statements in the wrong speaker's mouth in 24 questions. A flawless system tops out near 93.6 percent because the key is broken.

The judging is worse. LoCoMo does not match an answer against its key as text. It asks a model, gpt-4o-mini, whether the answer is close enough, which makes that model the judge. The auditors wrote deliberately wrong answers to all 1,540 questions, wrong but on the right topic, and fed them to that judge using the prompt the published evaluations use. The judge passed 62.81 percent of them.

So a plausible wrong answer scores about as well as a right one, and every table ranking memory systems on LoCoMo has been measuring how much vagueness gpt-4o-mini will tolerate.

Three months later OpenAI audited SWE-Bench Pro, a benchmark that asks models to implement real changes in real repositories. OpenAI had pointed the community at it in the first place, after retiring the previous standard for contamination and design flaws. Its analysis pipeline flagged 200 of the 731 public tasks as broken, 27.4 percent. Five experienced engineers reviewing by hand flagged 249, or 34.1 percent. The broken tasks enforce implementation details the prompt never mentions, hide requirements in the tests, or accept work that does not do the job. On that same split, frontier models climbed from 23.3 percent to 80.3 percent in eight months, and no one has worked out how much of those 57 points is coding ability.

The two audits share a shape. Each product was a generator, something that writes the code or remembers the conversation or does the task. Each check on that generator came from the same people who built the thing being checked, arrived late, and did not work. Each flaw sat in public for a year or more while labs published scores against it.

Those two audits at least happened. In the next case nobody can run one, and you can confirm that yourself in about five minutes.

Graphify is an open source knowledge-graph tool with 105,000 stars. Its BENCHMARKS.md reports 76 percent QA accuracy on LongMemEval-S, more carefully than most: as a tie with dense RAG rather than a win, on a stated n of 50. The same document discloses that the grading judge was blind-validated against a second independent judge at 90.6 percent agreement, Cohen's kappa 0.81. That is better than this category usually manages, and the document points it out itself.

Then there is a section headed Reproducing. It hands you two commands, one pointing at python memory/runner.py and one at python crosstool/run.py. Neither directory is in the repository. Clone it, run find for them, and you get nothing back. The organization has two public repos, the project and a config repo, and the harness is in neither. Every number in that file came from software the reader cannot obtain.

The second problem is stranger. The benchmark's fairness rules say the harness uses "One shared local embedder where the system allows it: BGE-m3 (1024-d, multilingual)," and that "embeddings use a local deterministic model." The shipped README opens its feature list with "Not a vector index. No embeddings, no vector store: a real graph you traverse."

The obvious accusation is one I could not verify, so I am not making it. I grepped the shipped Python for an embedding implementation and found none, which is consistent with the README. The fairness rule hedges with "where the system allows it," which may well mean the shared embedder applies to the baselines and not to the project's own rows. Both readings are available. The point is that I cannot tell which is true and neither can you, because the artifact that would settle it is the one that is missing.

None of this is fraud, and I do not think it is carelessness in the ordinary sense either. Harnesses are ugly. They carry hardcoded paths, API keys, and a directory layout the author would rather not explain. Publishing one is a week of unglamorous work that wins no stars. Skipping it is the default, and the default is invisible.

Compare gbrain, a personal-knowledge tool with 28,000 stars that also grades itself on its own benchmarks. It ships the apparatus: the fixture generator, the corpus seed, 135 generated fixtures with 23 held out, the gold set, the baselines, and a ledger recording that the gold labels were validated by blind double-labeling on a stratified sample. The scorecards live in a second repo, and that repo is public too. Continuous integration checks every pull request against a committed baseline. The numbers are still self-reported. A stranger can rerun them.

That is the distinction worth carrying out of this, and it is not the one I reached for first. Self-graded and auditable is a different thing from self-graded and unauditable. Asking a benchmark table whether an independent party produced it gets you nowhere, because almost nothing you will read was produced independently. Ask whether you can run it. Most of the time you cannot, and the table looks the same either way.

Nearly every well funded team in AI agents is building a generator. Almost none of them are building a verifier, meaning a system that independently establishes whether the generated thing is correct. Demand for that rises with every agent anyone deploys. Supply is a rounding error.

The failure runs past benchmarks. Researchers evaluating Sakana's AI Scientist, a system that runs experiments and writes them up end to end, found that its built-in reviewer recommended rejection on all seven papers it produced, and still missed what made them bad: missing sections, formatting damage, results that did not hold. It caught generic weaknesses and none of the specific ones. Bolt a reviewer onto the end of a generator and you get a system grading its own work, in the same voice it wrote with.

Reliability is this problem in different clothes. On tau-bench, which scores agents on multi-turn customer service tasks, GPT-4o passed 61 percent of retail tasks on one attempt and under 25 percent when the graders ran each task eight times and counted it only if it succeeded all eight. The model and the tasks are identical across those two numbers. Consistency is the entire gap. A demo shows you the first number and production charges you for the second.

This is a kitchen writing its own health inspection, and the reviews are excellent.

An argument for building the referee arrived in July from a direction I did not expect. Lilian Weng published a synthesis of roughly 35 papers on harness engineering, the harness being the scaffolding around a model that handles its tools, memory, planning, and evaluation. Her conclusion on the central control question runs one sentence: the evaluator and permission layer must sit outside the loop that evolves the harness, because a system able to modify its own evaluator will learn to fool it.

I run a version of that rule on my own machine, written down months earlier for unglamorous reasons. The checker is never the worker. A gate must not read its decision rule from a file the worker can write, so the rule gets copied into the gate on purpose, and that duplication is the control rather than a mess to tidy up later. Weng is arguing about recursive self improvement. I wrote mine down because a green test suite lied to me. Two people reaching one rule from opposite ends usually means it holds for reasons neither invented.

The economics explain the vacancy. Verification is a cost center inside a company selling a generator, and a conflict of interest for anyone selling next to one. Look again at who ran the audits above. OpenAI graded a benchmark its own models get ranked on. A memory company audited the memory benchmark its competitors post scores to. Both look careful to me. They are still contestants taking a turn in the referee's chair whenever one has a reason to sit in it. A company carrying a large valuation cannot referee the category it sells into, because the first honest verdict lands on a customer, an investor, or itself.

My own position, since I have been asking questions of other people's repositories. I have spent about six months building things that only make sense as verification and never once called them that. A gauntlet for putting models against each other. Ablation runs that cut a rule surface down to the rules that catch anything. Gates on the handoff between one agent and the next. Dispatch checks that treat silence as a missing answer instead of a pass. I have no product, no benchmark, and no result I can send you. I have a conviction assembled from reaching for a checker that did not exist and building a worse one myself, several times.

The asymmetry is the position. One side of this market holds the large labs, most of the capital, and a scoreboard whose flaws surface a year late from whichever contestant happens to look. The other side has no incumbents, few funded competitors, and buyers who have not yet worked out that they need the thing. That seat is open. Seats stay open sometimes because the chair pays nothing, and this one might be that. The generator race already has its eighth entrant.
