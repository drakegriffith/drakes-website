The Pledge

A blog hub where anyone can post. Nobody is allowed to use AI.

I build AI systems for a living. I have read more model output this year than most people will read in a lifetime, and I can tell you what it costs: the internet now has an infinite supply of writing and almost no supply of anyone having actually thought about something.

So here is the other thing. A hub. Anyone can publish. The only rule is that you wrote it.

The pledge itself

  1. I wrote this myself. All of it. Including the bad sentence in paragraph three.
  2. No model drafted it, outlined it, "polished" it, or suggested the ending.
  3. Spellcheck is fine. Spellcheck has been fine since 1993.
  4. If I quote a machine, I say so, and I put it in quotes.
  5. If I break this, I take the post down myself before anyone has to ask.

That is the whole contract. It is shorter than every terms of service you have ever agreed to, and unlike those, someone actually reads it.

How it gets enforced

An honor system with no teeth is a mood board. So every submission runs through a detector before it goes live. It scores the usual tells, the ones the essay graders have been scoring for two years now, because there is no reason to invent this from scratch:

  • Burstiness. Humans write a nineteen word sentence and then a short one. Models write nineteen, nineteen, nineteen.
  • Perplexity. If every single word is the most predictable next word, something predicted it.
  • Vocabulary tells. Delve, tapestry, testament, "it's not just X, it's Y," three-item lists forever.
  • Structure tells. Every section the same length. Every paragraph a topic sentence. A conclusion that restates the introduction and adds nothing.
  • The tidiness problem. Real writing has a loose end in it. Slop resolves completely, every time, because nothing was ever actually at stake.

Two things I will say up front, because the alternative is pretending.

First, the detector will be wrong sometimes. Every one of them is. A careful writer with clean prose can trip it, and a determined faker can beat it. So it does not delete anything and it does not get a vote. It flags, a human reads, and a human decides. I wrote a whole post about what happens when you let a model be the judge: my best-tuned reviewer flagged 82 percent of specs when 35 percent were actually bad, which means a rubber stamp that read nothing would have scored better. I am not making that mistake twice in the same year.

Second, yes, I am aware. A person who builds agent systems is starting an AI-free publishing hub, and the detector is software, and I will be pointing my own tools at the problem. I do not think that is hypocrisy. I think the machines are extremely good at some jobs and completely wrong for this one, and knowing the difference is the entire skill.

What you get

  • Your post, on the hub, in Times New Roman like God intended.
  • Search across everything anyone has written here, built on real retrieval instead of ten blue links sold to the highest bidder.
  • An MCP endpoint, so agents can read the hub properly instead of scraping it badly.
  • No engagement metrics. No recommended feed. No font change in two years.

Status
