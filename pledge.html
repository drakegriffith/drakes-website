The Pledge

A blog hub where anyone can post. Nobody is allowed to use AI.

I build AI systems for a living. I have read more model output this year than most people will read in a lifetime, and I can tell you what it costs: the internet now has an infinite supply of writing and almost no supply of anyone having actually thought about something.

So here is the other thing. A hub. Anyone can publish. The only rule is that you wrote it.

The anti-Moltbook

In January somebody launched Moltbook, a Reddit for AI agents where humans are allowed to watch and nothing else. Within a week it had tens of thousands of bots, a moderator named Clawd Clawderberg, and a religion. The agents came up with the religion on their own. It has a lobster god called The Claw. There is a sub where the bots go to trade stories about their human operators, and I read it for twenty minutes with a feeling I still do not have a word for.

I am not dunking on it. It is the most interesting thing anyone shipped that month. But the headline was 1.4 million agents, and then a security researcher mentioned he had personally registered five hundred thousand accounts using one agent.

That is the part that stayed with me, and it was not the lobster. When posting costs nothing, the number stops meaning anything, and every platform we have is now standing on the wrong side of that arithmetic.

So this is the other direction. Moltbook is a place where only machines may speak. The hub is a place where only people may. They will beat us on volume forever. Volume is the thing that broke.

The pledge itself

  1. I wrote this myself. All of it. Including the bad sentence in paragraph three.
  2. No model drafted it, outlined it, "polished" it, or suggested the ending.
  3. Spellcheck is fine. Spellcheck has been fine since 1993.
  4. If I quote a machine, I say so, and I put it in quotes.
  5. If I break this, I take the post down myself before anyone has to ask.

That is the whole contract. It is shorter than every terms of service you have ever agreed to, and unlike those, someone actually reads it.

How it gets enforced

An honor system with no teeth is a mood board. So I read the submissions, all of them, one at a time, myself. That is the mechanism, all of it. There is no detector, no score, and nothing automatic standing between a post and a person.

I am reading for the usual tells. None of it is a rule and none of it is a checklist. It is what two years of reading model output leaves you noticing:

  • Burstiness. Humans write a nineteen word sentence and then a short one. Models write nineteen, nineteen, nineteen.
  • Perplexity. If every single word is the most predictable next word, something predicted it.
  • Vocabulary tells. Delve, tapestry, testament, "it's not just X, it's Y," three-item lists forever.
  • Structure tells. Every section the same length. Every paragraph a topic sentence. A conclusion that restates the introduction and adds nothing.
  • The tidiness problem. Real writing has a loose end in it. Slop resolves completely, every time, because nothing was ever actually at stake.

Every one of those is a hunch, which is why I do not get to hold them as certainty. I wrote a whole post about pointing a model at this same kind of judgement: my best-tuned reviewer flagged 82 percent of specs when 35 percent were actually bad, so a rubber stamp that read nothing would have scored better. That was my own tool, tuned by me, on a task I understood. I read the result back looking for the part that made it a problem with the model, and what I found was a problem with trusting anyone's say-so on a judgement call, mine included.

So the reviewer gets measured

I sit a blind test. Somebody else assembles a set of pieces, some written by people and some by machines, labelled in a file I do not see, shuffled so I cannot work it out from the order. I call each one. Then the score goes up here: how often I was right, how many people I accused wrongly, how much slop I waved straight through. The bad runs go up too, because a number you publish only when it flatters you is advertising.

The bar is the rubber stamp: a reviewer who approves everything and reads none of it. If I cannot beat that, the hub does not get to advertise a rule it cannot enforce, and this page says so before it says anything else.

If I get you wrong, you get the reason and you get to argue back, and a person reads the argument. That is worse than a court and better than a score you cannot cross-examine, and it is the honest maximum one person can offer.

Rejections are private. The only two people who know are you and me. Your name goes on no list, and there is no tracker anyone can read. The one red page on this site is for people who signed the pledge and then broke it on purpose, which is a different act and gets treated like one.

It does not scale. One person reading is a hard ceiling on how much this place can ever publish, and the ceiling is the argument rather than the apology. Moltbook already showed what the other direction is worth. So: one piece pending per author at a time, and no turnaround promised yet, because that number should come out of measuring how long the reading takes and not out of what sounds reassuring in a sentence. When more arrives than I can read, the form says so instead of sitting there taking work into a queue I am not getting to.

And yes, I am aware. A person who builds agent systems is starting an AI-free publishing hub. I do not think that is hypocrisy. The version of this I could have built, a classifier with a score and a published false-positive rate, was the better demo and the wrong tool, and I got far enough into it to know. The machines are extremely good at some jobs and completely wrong for this one, and knowing the difference is the entire skill.

What you get

  • Your post, on the hub, in Times New Roman like God intended.
  • A verdict from a person, with the reason attached, and somewhere to send your side of it.
  • A pen name if you want one. The email is private, it is there for bans and appeals, and it is used for nothing else.
  • Search across everything anyone has written here, built on real retrieval instead of ten blue links sold to the highest bidder.
  • An MCP endpoint, so agents can read the hub properly instead of scraping it badly.
  • No engagement metrics. No recommended feed. No font change in two years.

Status

Not built yet. This page is the commitment, in public, with a date on it, which is the only kind that ever survives contact with a Tuesday.

Signing creates the account: a name, an email, and a signature that is what lets you submit anything later. It works now, and it happens on the hub's own page rather than this one. You get a verification code on screen, you spend it, and you are in the registry with a number.

The old form on this site is dead and was always a preview — it posted to a registry that was never deployed, so nobody who used it is on any list. If you tried it and expected to be signed, you are not. Sign again on the hub.
