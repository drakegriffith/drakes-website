I asked two AI models to destroy my work. They disagreed about how.

July 26, 2026

For the last few weeks I have been designing a piece of software, and before any decision gets locked I hand the write-up to two AI models and ask them to break it. Kimi and Codex. Same brief, sent in parallel. Neither can see the other's response, and neither remembers the last round.

The setup was meant to catch my mistakes, which it does. It also taught me something about reviewing that I did not go looking for.

Same brief, different reviewers

The important detail first, because it is what makes the rest meaningful: both models get the identical file. Same bytes. Same opening line telling them they are an adversarial reviewer and should default to skepticism. Neither one is told to be harsher than the other.

Here is what came back on the same document, on the same day.

  Kimi Codex
words 2,706 1,915
numeric claims 15 6
bullet points 31 88
average sentence length 31.8 words 31.9 words

Average sentence length matches to a tenth of a word, so neither is padding. What differs is where they go.

Kimi is the empiricist. It re-ran my measurement script, without being asked to, and compared its own output against the figures I had published. That is how it found the error: I had been counting text written by AI subagents as though a human had typed it. I published 10.6 percent as 14.4 percent. It then quoted my own script's documentation back at me, which had warned against exactly that mistake. I had written the warning and then ignored it.

Codex is the structuralist. It went looking for my experiments folder before reading anything, and attacked the shape of my argument rather than its arithmetic. Its finding was that my headline metric multiplied five rates measured over four different populations. The arithmetic was correct. The number meant nothing.

Then both of them opened with the same two objections. They run in parallel, memoryless, unable to coordinate. That agreement carried more weight than either review alone, and it convinced me to rebuild the document rather than patch it.

The two days I spent trying to make one of them fair

Kimi has a habit that took me a while to understand. It never stops. Every round produces fresh criticism, all of it individually defensible, and I could keep revising forever without ever arriving at "this is finished."

For a while I read that as temperament, so I tried to fix it the way everyone tries to fix it. I rewrote the instructions.

I ran this properly, over three days in July, with a sealed holdout set and a pre-registered decision rule. The question was narrow: can any wording make this model a useful pass-or-fail gate on a specification? I gave it more context, then less. I told it to be harsher. I told it to be gentler. I moved the stated failure rate up and down, and the stated cost of missing something.

The answer was no, and the shape of the no is the part worth having.

It flagged 82 percent of specifications when 35 percent of them were actually bad. A rubber stamp that read nothing and approved everything scored better than every prompt I wrote. On obviously good specs and obviously terrible ones it was perfect. On real ones it was a coin flip.

Every tone lever moved the flag rate and none of them improved the accuracy. Tilting it toward flagging produced nine false alarms and zero real catches. Its sense of what looks risky turns out to be uncorrelated with what is actually broken. This is the finding I keep coming back to, because "just tell it to be more critical" is the first thing anyone reaches for and it is worse than useless. It produces more noise that feels like more rigour.

More instruction made it monotonically worse. Every added distinction raised its flagging without adding a single new catch. One rewrite collapsed it into approving everything.

My best configuration scored 44 out of 50 on the data I tuned it against, then caught zero of three real failures on the sealed holdout. It had memorised the phrasing of the examples I let it see rather than learning anything.

I filed the whole thing as a failed experiment and moved on.

It was not a failed experiment

Three days later I was writing up the review process and realised the null result was the load-bearing part.

The model that loses to a rubber stamp on pass-or-fail is the same model that re-ran my script and caught a real error in my published numbers. Both of those are true at once, and I had been treating them as one purchase.

Finding things and judging things are separate skills, and this reviewer has one of them.

So the process I ended up with never asks a model for a verdict. It asks for findings, and every finding has to cite a line, a number, or a specific inference. Anything that cannot point at something is advisory by default. Findings get sorted into fatal, material, and advisory, and only fatal blocks acceptance.

Review stops when a round produces nothing fatal, with a hard cap of three rounds. And the rule that actually made it terminate: later rounds may only check whether earlier findings were resolved. They may not open new ground. New ground becomes a separate task.

Without that last rule, every round legitimately discovers something new, because any document is infinitely criticisable, and the sequence never ends. With it, the loop is bounded by the first round's findings, which is a finite list. The reviewer stopped being the thing that decides when I am done, which was the real problem all along. Before, that decision was made by whatever hour I ran out of patience.

A postscript about losing my own work

While writing this I could not find the three-day experiment I just described. It was on my own laptop, committed to git, in a folder two directories from where I was working, finished the previous week.

I searched for "prompt engineering." I searched for "tone" across 3,483 conversation transcripts. I ran a full content search across every project folder for "harsh," "be kind," and half a dozen similar phrases. All of it came back empty, or came back with results about email etiquette.

The document that answered the question was sitting right there. It just never uses those words. It says propensity levers, base rate, cost asymmetry, flag rate. I had named the work for what it measured, and I went looking for it using the words I would have described it with months later. Both descriptions are correct. They share no vocabulary.

What eventually found it was listing my folders by date and reading the names.

Nothing was lost, corrupted, or missing. Storage was perfect. Retrieval failed completely, on the gap between how I filed something and how I later asked for it. That is a more honest argument for better search than any benchmark I could have designed, and I got it by accident, from failing at it.

Two caveats, because they matter

The prompt was identical for both models. The surrounding software was not: each runs through its own command line tool with its own defaults. The comparison is controlled on instructions and imperfectly controlled on environment, and a cleaner version of this would hold both fixed.

The calibration result is about one model at one task, gating specifications. It is not a claim about that model generally, and it is certainly not a claim about the others. What generalises is the method: measure your reviewer against a baseline that does nothing before you trust its verdicts, because a reviewer that flags most things can look rigorous while carrying no information at all.
